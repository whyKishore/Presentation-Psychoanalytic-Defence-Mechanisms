## Introduction

Psychoanalytic theory explains some behaviour as a response to unconscious conflict and anxiety. This paper outlines Freud's account of anxiety and ego defences, introduces related ideas from Karen Horney and Erik Erikson, and considers how these frameworks might be used to think about social-media behaviour.

The social-media examples are the author's illustrations, not findings from the readings or evidence that a particular online behaviour has a specific psychological cause. They should be treated as possibilities for reflection, not as diagnoses.

## 1. Freud: Personality, Conflict, and Anxiety

In Freud's structural model, the mind is understood through three interacting systems:

- **Id:** the source of instinctual wishes and impulses, operating according to the pleasure principle.
- **Ego:** the part that negotiates between the id, the superego, and external reality, operating according to the reality principle.
- **Superego:** the internalised standards and ideals associated with conscience and morality.

Freud described anxiety as a signal that the ego may be facing danger or conflict. He distinguished:

- **Objective (or realistic) anxiety:** fear in response to an external, real-world danger.
- **Neurotic anxiety:** fear that an impulse may lead to punishment or other consequences.
- **Moral anxiety:** guilt or shame arising from conflict with the superego.

In this account, the ego uses **defence mechanisms**, largely unconsciously, to manage anxiety and internal conflict.

## 2. Defence Mechanisms

Anna Freud developed the study of defence mechanisms as unconscious strategies used by the ego to manage anxiety. In the course reading, these mechanisms are presented as ways of reducing tension, particularly tension associated with conflict between the id and superego. Recognising them can support reflection and self-awareness, though a behaviour alone is not enough to establish that a defence mechanism is operating.

The following summaries and everyday examples are drawn from the lesson.

| Mechanism | Meaning | Example from the lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Repression** | Unconsciously keeping a distressing memory, wish, or impulse out of conscious awareness. | A person who experienced abuse in childhood may be unable to recall the incident. |
| **Denial** | Refusing to acknowledge a distressing reality or its emotional significance. | A person with terminal cancer refuses to accept that they may not recover. |
| **Displacement** | Redirecting an impulse or feeling from its original, risky target to a safer substitute. | A person harassed by a supervisor takes out their anger on family members at home. |
| **Projection** | Attributing one's own unacceptable feeling or impulse to someone else. | A woman troubled by her own desire to be unfaithful accuses her husband of infidelity. |
| **Rationalization** | Offering a plausible-sounding justification that obscures the more troubling reason for an action. | A student caught plagiarising blames unfair exams and says everyone else was doing it. |
| **Intellectualization** | Focusing on abstract or analytical aspects of a distressing situation rather than engaging with its emotional impact. | After a rejection, a person gives a persuasive account of the unpredictability of romantic relationships. |
| **Reaction formation** | Expressing an exaggerated attitude or behaviour that is the opposite of an unacceptable feeling or wish. | A mother who feels animosity towards her child behaves with excessive caution and protectiveness. |
| **Sublimation** | Channeling an impulse into an activity that is socially accepted or valued. | An antagonistic investigative reporter uses their work to expose wrongdoing and influence political careers. |
| **Regression** | Reverting to an earlier, more dependent or childlike way of responding when under stress. | The lesson does not provide an example. |

These are theoretical descriptions, not labels to apply automatically. Similar actions can have different explanations, and context matters.

## 3. Other Psychoanalytic Perspectives

### 3.1 Karen Horney: Anxiety in Social Relationships

Horney shifted emphasis from biological drives towards the social relationships and conditions in which personality develops.

- **Basic anxiety** is a child's sense of isolation and helplessness in a potentially hostile world. In Horney's account, it can develop in response to experiences such as parental dominance, lack of affection, or inconsistent treatment; it is not innate.
- **Neurotic needs** are strategies for coping with basic anxiety that become rigid or persistent. Horney grouped them into three broad orientations: **moving toward people** (compliant), **moving against people** (aggressive), and **moving away from people** (detached).
- **Idealised self-image** is an unrealistic picture of the self that obscures inner conflict and may nevertheless be sincerely believed.

### 3.2 Erik Erikson: Identity Across the Lifespan

Erikson proposed a psychosocial theory of development across the lifespan, giving particular importance to social and cultural influences. His theory describes eight psychosocial tensions, with different ones becoming especially prominent at different stages of life. Two are relevant to the discussion here:

- **Identity vs. role confusion** (adolescence): developing a coherent sense of identity. Erikson introduced the idea of an “identity crisis.”
- **Intimacy vs. isolation** (young adulthood): forming close relationships while maintaining and sharing a sense of self.

Erikson's framework is related to psychoanalytic thought but places development in a broader social context.

## 4. Applying These Ideas to Social Media

The course reading does not examine social media. The connections below are therefore the author's interpretations: they show how the theories might prompt questions about online behaviour, not what causes that behaviour.

- **Horney's idealised self-image:** Might a carefully curated profile help someone maintain an idealised picture of themselves?
- **Horney's compliant orientation:** Could repeated approval-seeking online be understood, in some cases, as an effort to move toward others and secure acceptance?
- **Erikson's account of identity:** Might online spaces provide one setting in which people explore and present aspects of identity?
- **Freud's account of defences:** Could an online interaction that creates anxiety be followed by a response resembling one of the mechanisms described above?

### Illustrative Online Scenarios

The scenarios below extend the lesson's definitions to social media. They are hypothetical and should not be used to infer another person's motives or mental state.

| Mechanism | Possible social-media illustration |
|---|---|
| **Repression** | Someone has little conscious awareness of a distressing online experience. This alone would not establish repression or explain why the memory is difficult to access. |
| **Denial** | A person dismisses the emotional impact of repeated online harassment, despite signs that it is distressing. |
| **Displacement** | After an upsetting offline conflict, a person directs their anger at strangers in a comment section. |
| **Projection** | Someone who is uncomfortable with their own wish for attention accuses other users of seeking attention. |
| **Rationalization** | A person justifies posting a hurtful comment by saying that the target deserved it or that other users post worse things. |
| **Intellectualization** | After an online rejection, someone posts detached analysis of relationships rather than engaging with their own feelings. |
| **Reaction formation** | A person makes conspicuously effusive public gestures toward someone they privately resent. |
| **Sublimation** | Someone channels competitive or hostile energy into constructive investigative, advocacy, or educational content. |
| **Regression** | Under stress from online criticism, someone responds in a markedly more dependent or childlike way, such as repeatedly seeking reassurance. |

## 5. Conclusion and Critical Evaluation

In Freud's model, anxiety arising from conflict among the id, ego, and superego can lead the ego to use unconscious defences. Horney placed greater emphasis on social relationships as a source of anxiety, while Erikson described identity and other developmental tensions across the lifespan. Together, these perspectives offer concepts for reflecting on how people manage conflict, relationships, and self-presentation.

The framework also has limitations. The readings note that psychoanalytic explanations rely substantially on clinical observation rather than laboratory experimentation, that Freud's biological emphasis was challenged by anthropology and sociology, and that psychoanalysis's present influence is debated. Most importantly for this discussion, the readings do not provide research on social media. Applying these theories online is therefore interpretive; it would require empirical research to establish whether, when, or how the proposed connections hold.
